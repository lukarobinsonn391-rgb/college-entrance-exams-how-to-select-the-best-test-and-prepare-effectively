<h1><strong>College Entrance Exams: How to Select the Best Test and Prepare Effectively</strong></h1>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Choosing a college entrance exam starts with one question: do the schools on your list require a test score, and which tests do they accept?&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">For most US applicants the answer is the SAT or the ACT, and in the 2026-27 admissions cycle the answer matters more than it has in years.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Six of the eight Ivy League schools, along with MIT, Stanford, Caltech, Johns Hopkins, and Georgetown, now require scores, while many other colleges remain test-optional.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This guide helps you decide whether to test, pick between the SAT and ACT, and build a prep plan that fits your timeline.</span></p>
<h2><strong>1. Start With the Big Picture: Do You Need a Test?</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Testing rules are changing quickly, and the trend runs toward requiring scores. After years of test-optional policies, several selective colleges have brought requirements back.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Reports from admissions firms say Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth, Brown, Cornell, and the University of Pennsylvania require SAT or ACT scores for 2026-27.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">With Princeton and Columbia still test-optional this cycle and announced to require scores beginning in 2027-28, though some sources differ on the details, so confirm each school directly.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">At the same time, a meaningful number of colleges remain test-optional. A Collegewise summary reported that over 1,000 colleges on the Common App were test-optional as of June 2026.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">"Test-optional" doesn't always mean "scores don't matter." At selective schools, a strong score can support your grades, and a weak one can raise questions.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Even where tests aren't required, scores may help with merit scholarships, honors programs, and placement.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Build your list first, then check each school's current policy on its own admissions page. If you decide to test, you'll also face a choice about how to prepare.&nbsp;</span></p>

<h2><strong>4. SAT vs. ACT vs. CLT: What to Know About Each</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Digital SAT. The SAT is taken on a computer and is adaptive. How you perform on the first module in each section determines the difficulty of the second.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">It has two sections, Reading and Writing and Math, with a total score from 400 to 1600. The test runs a little over two hours, and calculators are allowed throughout the Math section.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Before choosing your preparation strategy, explore the College Board's</span><a href="https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/practice"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">official SAT practice resources</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">These materials help you understand the digital exam format, become familiar with question types, and practise the skills you'll need on test day.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Enhanced ACT. The ACT rolled out a shorter format in 2025 and 2026. The core test covers English, Math, and Reading, with 131 questions and roughly two hours of testing time. The Science section is now optional.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The composite score still runs from 1 to 36, and it reflects only the sections you take. The Math section also moved to four answer choices instead of five.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Because the format varies by test date and state during the rollout, check the ACT's official website for details that apply to you.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Classic Learning Test (CLT). A smaller alternative accepted by a limited set of colleges, the CLT emphasizes reading and reasoning with classic texts.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">How do you decide? If you prefer adaptive testing and data-driven reasoning, the SAT may fit your needs.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If you prefer a quicker pace, the ACT might be a better choice. Your diagnostic scores can help you decide which test suits your strengths.</span></p>

<h2><strong>6. Build a Study Plan That Works</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A good plan has structure. Try this:</span></p>
<ol>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Set a target score.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Look at the middle 50% score range at each school on your list.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Find the gap.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Compare your diagnosis to your target and see which sections need the most work.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Schedule short, frequent sessions.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Four or five sessions a week of 45 to 90 minutes beats one weekend marathon.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Learn the content, then practice timed.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Review concepts first, then do mixed sets under time limits.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Keep an error log.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Record why you missed each question: content gap, careless mistake, or timing. Patterns show you what to fix.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Take full-length practice tests.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Do these regularly, under real conditions, using official materials from the College Board or ACT when possible.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Review every test.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Reviewing is where most of the improvement happens.</span></li>
</ol>
<h2><strong>7. Choose Your Prep Resources</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">You can combine free and paid resources. Good options include:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Official practice tests.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> The College Board and ACT publish real or realistic tests, which give the most accurate view of the exam.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Free tools.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Many libraries, schools, and nonprofits provide free practice and tutoring.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Online platforms.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Structured programs provide lessons, question banks, and score tracking.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Tutors.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> One-on-one help works well for targeted problems, though it costs more.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>School support.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Ask your counselor about prep classes and fee waivers.</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Check that any resource is built for the current Digital SAT or Enhanced ACT, not an older version, because pacing and question counts changed.</span></p>
<h2><strong>8. Test-Day Strategy</strong></h2>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Prepare the night before.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Pack your admission ticket, ID, approved calculator, and charged device if needed.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Sleep well.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Cramming the night before rarely helps.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Pace yourself.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Don't spend too long on one question, and mark it to return.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Answer every question.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Neither exam penalizes wrong answers.</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><strong>Stay calm.</strong><span style="font-weight: 400;"> Brief breathing exercises can steady you between sections.</span></li>
</ul>
<h2><strong>9. Retakes, Superscoring, and Deadlines</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Many students take a test more than once. Many colleges superscore, combining your best section scores across dates, though policies vary, so check each school.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Plan your final test date well before application deadlines so scores arrive in time, and remember that early applications have earlier deadlines.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fee waivers are available for eligible students, so ask your counselor early.</span></p>
